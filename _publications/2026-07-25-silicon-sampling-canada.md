Granular data on citizens is everywhere, and large language models (LLMs) can now answer survey questions on their behalf at negligible cost — a practice known as **silicon sampling**. The combination promises much for political science and risks much for democratic politics. For scholars, it can fill missing data, cut fieldwork costs, and reach hard-to-survey populations; for political operatives, it can pre-test microtargeting on simulated electorates at a fraction of the cost and time.

But the very training that gives LLMs this reach may compress out what matters. At the aggregate level, they already succeed; at the individual level, they may not — and it is the mechanism that both scholarly imputation and microtargeting actually need. Hence our question: **do LLMs reproduce the theoretical mechanisms underlying vote choice, or merely stereotypes absorbed during training?**

We test this on the 2021 Canadian Election Study. For each of three theoretical frameworks from the Canadian voting behaviour literature — sociodemographic characteristics, left–right ideology, and issue voting — we compute average marginal effects from a multinomial logit and compare them to counterfactual predictions from three LLMs. Canada is a demanding test: multiparty system, linguistic cleavage, and Bloc Québécois are poorly represented in the predominantly American training corpora. Beyond validating silicon sampling for non-American, multiparty contexts, the findings speak to the political stakes of making electorates cheap to simulate.
